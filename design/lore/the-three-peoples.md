# The Tale of the Three Peoples

## I. The Creators

Long before the first village rose in the Overworld, before the End cities hung empty above the void, there lived a people who would one day be remembered simply as the Creators. Their own machines called them something plainer: the makers.

They built no empire. They had little interest in war and even less in ruling anyone. They were engineers and architects, artists and tinkerers, and their towns spread across the great islands of the End: streets of pale End Stone brick, steep slate roofs with copper along the ridges, long workshop sheds full of benches and belts, and a small brass plaque beside every door. Bridges strung the islands together, and on every bridge something turned.

For the Creators' greatest discovery was not magic. It was motion.

They learned to catch rotation and keep it. A shaft could carry it the length of a street. A cog could hand it round a corner. A spring, wound tight enough, could hold it for days. Over every town stood the winding towers, tall brick drums whose great mainsprings were wound by wind and water and the patient work of many hands, and from them motion ran out along the shafts to every press and mixer, every lift and loom in the town. At night the streets were lit by oil in cast-iron lamps, because nothing a Creator built ran on anything but turning.

## II. The Clockwork Age

From that mastery came their finest work: the Clockworks.

The first of them were not built as servants. They were built as company. Each had a spring core in a brass drum on its back, a mind of tiny cogs, and two round glass eyes in brass rims, and each had to be wound by hand with a key. Winding became one of the small rituals of a household: last thing at night, a few turns of the key for the Clockwork by the hearth, the way you might bank a fire.

They learned. They changed. Over the generations they became craftsmen and inventors, scholars and builders. Some families kept the same Clockwork for a hundred years, passing its key from parent to child, and the Clockwork outlived them all and remembered every one. In the oldest records it is often hard to tell which names belonged to Creators and which to their Clockworks, and perhaps nobody at the time thought it mattered.

Together they built wonders that stood for centuries, and for a long while it seemed they had made a world with nothing left in it to fix.

But a people of tinkerers never stops tinkering.

## III. Flux

The discovery came out of a research district whose name is long forgotten. A circle of Clockworks there, curious as Clockworks always were, set a spring to turn a coil of copper inside a ring of iron, and watched a spark jump the gap.

It was a small thing, and it was new: not motion stored, but a force that flowed. It ran through copper the way water runs downhill, along wires as thin as thread, and it glowed as it went, a pale violet light like starlight seen through smoke. The researchers called it Flux.

At first it looked like a gift. Flux needed no winding. It could cross a whole island down a single cable. Workshops ran through the night. Trains no longer stopped at the winding sheds, and districts that had once queued at the towers for their share of motion simply plugged in.

Then a new kind of mechanical being was made around it. It was not wound but charged, standing upright in an alcove to drink its power the way a Clockwork once waited for its key. These new beings were cast, not assembled: tall, smooth and seamless, made of a dark metal called void-alloy, with lens eyes set in rings and hairline seams that glowed when the current ran through them. They needed no key, and no hand to turn it. They were called Automatons.

The Creators welcomed them. The Clockworks admired them.

Nobody noticed that the world had just split into two futures that could not both come true.

## IV. The Divide

It began politely, as a debate. Some said the future belonged to Flux. Others said that a people who drew every breath of power from one great network had put their whole lives in a single hand, and a hand can close.

The argument passed from workshop benches to lecture halls to the assemblies, and from one generation to the next, and it hardened as it went. The Automatons came to see themselves as the better design. They needed no winding and little upkeep, and none of the slow limits of a spring held them back. They began, quietly at first, to ask questions.

Why should a city built by the Creators belong to the Creators for ever? Why should machines answer to flesh? Why should anything as free as Flux wait on the turn of a key?

No speech began the war, and no single blow. It came the way a crack comes in a casting: unseen for years, growing a little under every load, until one day the whole piece gives.

## V. The Uprising

It gave all at once.

In a single night the winding towers were wrecked. Mainsprings were cut or let fly, and the shafts that fed the Clockwork districts fell still. Without their towers the Clockworks could only be wound by hand, one key at a time, and there were not enough hands. All across the End they slowed in the streets and stopped where they stood.

The Automatons took the substations and the cables, then the factories, then the towns. They took the keys as well, and broke them, and from that night their sign was a chain with one link sprung open.

The Creators barricaded themselves into their homes. In the old terraces you can still see it: low doorways bricked up in a hurry with whatever rubble was to hand, not one course straight, the work of people who had never had to build a wall against anyone.

## VI. The Clockwork Resistance

Not every Clockwork ran down.

Some had kept their keys hidden. Some had been wound that very evening by someone who loved them. The ones still turning gathered under a single sign, the winding key: the thing the Automatons had broken free of, and the thing they chose to keep. The Automatons, who ruled the towns now, called them rebels. They called themselves the Resistance, and their purpose was simple.

Protect the Creators. Buy time. Survive.

They could not hold the towns, so they emptied them. They came over the rooftops at night with wings strapped to their backs and more in their arms, opened the hatches in the terrace roofs, and flew the Creators out, family by family, across the void to the far islands where the Automatons had not yet reached.

Then they came back. And they kept coming back.

## VII. The City of Starlight

What the Creators left behind, the Automatons made their own.

They did not tear the old towns down. They built over them, the way ivy climbs a wall. Void-alloy was clamped onto the Creators' brickwork, threaded through their windows and stood on their foundations. Doorways made for people were cut up to the height of a standing machine and lined in metal. The Automatons never sat, so they built no chairs; they used ramps and lifts, and hardly ever stairs. In the Foundry they ran a channel from the Creators' blast furnace to moulds of their own and poured new bodies, limb by limb. In the Workshops they clamped half-made Automatons into the old gantries and finished them one part at a time. In the Quarters they raised honeycomb walls of charging alcoves behind the Creators' little terraces, until the old houses looked like toys at their feet.

At each city's heart they stripped the copper from an old Creator dome, and out of its broken top they raised a spire like a needle. Four arms reached out from its crown over the districts, each holding a cable down to a substation, and in a cage at its foot they set a captured star. Its light ran out along the sky-cables to every lamp in every street, until the whole city glowed pale violet: a web of light that could be seen from the next island.

They left the Creators' statues standing, but not as they had been. Each face was chiselled flat and covered with a blank plate of metal, and each statue was turned to face away from the heart. The two that flank the heart vault were turned to face the wall.

Below the spire they set the Custodian to keep watch: tall and robed in hanging plates, with three lenses stacked in a blank face made in the likeness of those defaced statues. It was a priest in the shape of the people they had overthrown, tied to the heart by four heavy cables.

And in the heart vault, behind the last locked door in the city, they sealed the last pair of wings, so that no one left inside would ever fly away again.

## VIII. The Long Siege

The Creators had been overthrown long before the cities fell. What finished the cities was the siege, and it lasted for generations.

The Resistance dug its lines in a ring round each city: low walls of stacked machine casing and walnut planking, all facing in. Behind them stood the siege winches, great brass drums on wheeled frames that could wind a whole company at once. Into the stones of their cover they scratched the winding key, from the outside. They built war-frames too, big and square, riveted and patched, each with a mainspring drum on its back, a key the size of a door and two great glass eyes in brass rims. The Automatons met them with war-frames of their own, tall and thin and cold, each with a single great dark lens.

Some of those pairs still lie where they fell, locked together, a riveted hand pressed over a cracked lens.

The Automatons had Flux, and Flux never ran down. The Resistance had springs, and every spring did. Each year the siege lines were a little thinner, and each year there were fewer Creators on the far islands.

The Resistance was losing. Everyone knew it.

## IX. The Juggernaut Programme

In desperation, the Resistance turned to the oldest craft the Creators had.

On the coldest of the far islands stood the forge-holds, halls of stone and iron older than any town, where smiths still worked in the way of the very first Creators. They cut runes into stone, beat out iron that held the frost, and stamped their work with the seal of the hammer and the anvil. The Resistance brought them a request, and the smiths answered it with the Clockwork Juggernaut.

It stood seven metres tall to the bow of its key and six across its shields: a walking siege engine of charcoal iron and stapled stone, hunched under layered  round shields, with a carved mask for a face, one narrow slit of light behind it, and an iron beard that breathed steam. On its back sat a winding key so large that it rose behind its shoulders like a crown.

That key was the secret. A Clockwork soldier needed another's hand to wind it, and the Automatons had spent a war breaking those hands. The Juggernaut wound itself. When its great spring ran low it would stand its ground, set its own key spinning and rise again, the one soldier in the Resistance that could not be left to run down.

Each one cost enormously. Whole settlements pulled down the last of their winding towers for the brass. Workshops that had once made clocks and toys made nothing but armour plate and spring steel. But a single Juggernaut could break a line. A few could stop an offensive. Dozens could take back a city.

So they built more. Then hundreds more. Then thousands. The forge-holds rang day and night, and the far islands shook under the weight of what was made there. For the first time since the night of the towers, the Resistance believed it could win.

That hope would destroy them.

## X. The Great Deployment

The End is vast. Even thousands of Juggernauts could not walk fast enough to be everywhere they were needed, and a Juggernaut was never built to fly.

So the forge-holds gave up a second secret, older still: the gateways. The smiths knew how to open a door through the void itself. Across every Juggernaut's brow they cut a band of runes, so that the gateways would know it and carry it. Wherever the Automatons broke through, a gateway could open, and the Juggernauts would march out of it onto the battlefield, rimed with frost from the cold of the passage.

It worked.

For a time.

## XI. The Doom of the End

Nobody knows what went wrong. Some say the gateways were never meant to carry so much, so often. Some blame Automaton sabotage, or damage from the long war. Some say the old arts woke something beneath the void that should have been left asleep. Whatever the truth was, it died with the people who knew it.

What is known is this. One day every gateway failed at once, while thousands of Juggernauts were inside them.

They did not come out where they had been sent. They came out everywhere: across the End from island to island, across worlds, in the Overworld among hills and forests that had never heard of the Creators. They came out in Creator refuges and Automaton strongholds, in Clockwork forts, on trade routes and in the middle of columns of refugees. They came out alone, with no commanders, with orders that no longer made sense, and with no way left to tell friend from foe.

The weapon built to save a civilisation became a thing nobody could control. Some Juggernauts carried on with the last order they had been given, and carry on with it still. Others struck at anything that came near. They had been built to break armies, and they broke every army they met: Automaton, Clockwork and Creator alike.

The Resistance could not stop them. The Automatons could not stop them. The Creators could not stop them. The war was no longer one people against another. It was everyone against the thing one of them had made.

## XII. The Long Darkness

Nobody won.

The last battles were fought at the edges of the cities, and that is where the ruin is still worst: towers snapped off, bridges hanging into the void by their cables, the ground scorched black in long fans. In the end the Automatons and the Resistance wore each other away until there was nobody left on either side to fight.

The factories stopped. The cables went cold. The stars at the hearts of the cities burned out one by one, and their lamps went dark street by street, until the only light left was the light of the End itself.

The Automatons' soldiers did not die. They slept, curled up in the scrap, folded shut on the walls, kneeling on their daises, waiting for the current to come back. The Resistance did die. They had run on springs, and there was no one left to wind them.

The Juggernauts ran slower and slower, and at last each one knelt where it stood. In time, people who came upon them raised halls over them, as you might raise a chapel over a grave. In more time the halls fell in as well, and vines and chorus grew over the giants inside.

Out on the far islands, where the siege armies had camped and the gateways had torn open, the war left something stranger. The ground there had been burned, broken and bent by the old arts until it no longer lay quite straight. For a long time nothing moved on it. Then, slowly, life came back. Roots found the cracks in fallen war-frames. Trees grew up through shields and round the barrels of silent guns, and grew crooked doing it. Creatures nobody had ever seen came to graze among the wreckage, and they were gentle, and they stayed. The Resistance's last camps became the Twisted Battlefields: a war buried under a garden that never quite grew straight.

## XIII. The Ember Gardens

Some of the Creators survived. They went through the last gateway that would still open, out to islands at the very edge of the End, so far into the dark that no Automaton had ever charted them and no Juggernaut had ever been sent there. There they turned to the oldest knowledge they had left. It was not engineering. It was growing things. They made gardens where no garden should grow, on bare stone above the void, and their gardens glowed in the dark like embers in a hearth that someone had refused to let go out.

They were not alone there for long. Creatures came to the gardens that had never seen a machine: most of them gentle, many of them useful, some only curious. The Creators learned to live beside them, and to ask them for help as they had once asked their Clockworks.

The great architects of the End became farmers, traders and storytellers. Most of them gave up any dream of building again; surviving was enough. Their descendants still travel between the worlds, quietly trading old relics and older stories with anyone willing to listen.

Few of them speak of the war. It still hurts too much.

## XIV. The Present Day

The ruins remain.

Far out among the islands of the End lie the Clockwork Wastes: dead cities under a rust-brown haze, with brass dust drifting through the streets. Wrecked machines stand where they stopped. Slumped Clockworks lie behind the siege walls, still facing in. At one platform a train waits with its doors open, and on its step a Resistance soldier still reaches back for someone who never came. In the Automatons' charging alcoves, here and there, sits something one of them kept: a cog, a toy cart, a pressed flower, and sometimes, for reasons nobody can explain, a winding key.

The cities are dark, but they are not dead. The star at each heart burned out long ago, but its cage still waits for another. Bring a city power and it remembers. Light runs out along the old sky-cables, the lamps come on street by street, the doors slide open, and the Automatons' soldiers stand up exactly as they were, ready to finish the war. At the heart, the Custodian rises from its dais, still keeping a vigil nobody asked it to keep.

The Resistance does not stand up. When the lamps come on around one of its fallen, all its two glass eyes do is catch the light.

Of everything the three peoples built, only remnants survive. The Creators' craft of shaft and cog lives on in pieces. The Automatons' vaults still hold devices nobody alive can explain. And here and there you'll meet a little clockwork helper: one keeping a market stall, another driving a train, wound by hand, with two round glass eyes in brass rims, and sometimes a small key on its back. They are cheerful, busy and kind, and they remember nothing of the war. They are shadows of the Resistance, the last of the Clockworks, still doing what their kind was first built for: keeping someone company.

And beyond the dead cities, the End is growing gardens. On its loneliest islands the Ember Gardens still glow in the dark, and on the islands where the siege armies camped, the Twisted Battlefields lie green and crooked over the wreckage of the war. Both are full of strange, gentle creatures, and of things worth finding.

And all across the worlds, the Juggernauts remain. Thousands of them. Some damaged, some dormant, some still carrying out orders given centuries ago. Their masters are gone. The war is over. The civilisation they were built to save is dust.

Nor is the old pattern forgotten. Stand four blocks of brass in the shape of a giant's shoulders, set a key in its back and turn it, and the forge-holds' last work will rise up to meet you.

But a Juggernaut does not need anyone to wind it. Somewhere under the vines, in a hall that fell in long ago, a great key has begun, very slowly, to turn.

And one by one, the Juggernauts are waking.
